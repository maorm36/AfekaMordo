Five Stunning Places 02  /  Petra, Jordan
No. 02

Petra, a city carved from a cliff.

Jordan  ·  Ma'an Governorate  ·  30.33°N, 35.44°E

Al-Khazneh, the Treasury, framed by the last turn of the Siq.

01
The rose city

Hidden in the desert for centuries

Petra is an entire city cut directly into pink sandstone cliffs, built by the Nabataean people more than two thousand years ago and lost to the western world for almost a thousand. A Swiss traveler rediscovered it in 1812 by disguising himself as a Bedouin and asking, politely, if he could see “the ruins over the hill.” The ruins, it turned out, stretched for kilometers.

02
The Siq

A 1.2-kilometer slot of light

You do not walk up to Petra. You walk down, for over a kilometer, through a narrow crack in the earth called the Siq. The walls rise eighty meters above your head, twisting like a ribbon, and in places the sky narrows to a single blue line. And then, at the very last turn, without warning, the slot opens and the Treasury appears — a forty-meter-tall facade of pink pillars and carved figures that the Nabataeans scooped out of the living rock with chisels.

03
The Monastery

800 steps to an emptier view

Most visitors see the Treasury and turn back. The Monastery, almost the same size but hidden at the top of 800 stone-cut steps at the far end of the valley, has maybe a tenth of the crowd. You climb for an hour past Bedouin children selling bracelets, through a last narrow stair, and the cliff face opens onto a fifty-meter carved facade with a view that goes on until the horizon turns into haze.

04
Bedouin hospitality

Tea with three sugars

The Bedouin families who still live around Petra will wave you into a cave for tea the way other people would offer you a chair. Cardamom tea, three sugars, kept hot over a bed of coals. Conversations in broken English about football, goats, and which valley has the best light in the late afternoon. It is not a performance; it is simply how desert culture treats a stranger who made it this far.

05
Petra by night

A canyon lit by 1,500 candles

Three nights a week the Siq is lined with fifteen hundred paper-bag candles, and you walk the whole kilometer in silence by candlelight until you reach the Treasury, where a bedouin plays the rababah, a single-stringed desert violin, on the sand. It is genuinely one of the strangest and most quiet experiences you can have as a traveler — a two-thousand-year-old building that is still somehow expecting you.

“No pen can describe, no painter portray, the beauty of that scene.” John William Burgon, on Petra, 1845
