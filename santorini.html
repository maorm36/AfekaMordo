Five Stunning Places 04  /  Santorini, Greece
No. 04

Santorini, a volcano that tourists befriended.

Greece  ·  Cyclades  ·  36.39°N, 25.46°E

Oia at the far northern tip of the island, hanging over the caldera.

01
A broken island

The shape of a catastrophe

Santorini is what is left over after a volcano did something dramatic. Around 1600 BCE the island erupted in one of the largest eruptions in human history — loud enough, some historians believe, to be the real event behind the legend of Atlantis — and the whole center of the island collapsed into the sea. What remains is a ring-shaped cliff around a 400-meter-deep flooded crater. The villages sit on the very edge of the cliff, and the view looks directly down into the wound.

02
Oia and Fira

Two towns, one impossible view

Fira is the capital, halfway along the cliff, busy and bright. Oia (pronounced “ee-ah”) is at the far northern tip, quieter, made of more blue domes and fewer cars. Both towns are entirely whitewashed — the law requires it — and the effect, from the water, is of someone having carefully poured cream along the top of a cake. Staircases zigzag down the cliffs. Bougainvillea spills over every other wall. It is a postcard that has learned to live in three dimensions.

03
The sunset

A nightly ceremony of about two thousand people

Every evening, about an hour before sunset, an informal parade forms from Oia's castle ruins down through the narrow lanes, everyone looking for a ledge or a rooftop with a clear line to the horizon. There is no announcement. There does not need to be. When the sun actually touches the sea, the crowd goes quiet, and then, as the last red sliver disappears, a few people start to clap. The island has applauded the sunset every night for about fifty years. It does not feel silly yet.

04
Food & wine

Tomatoes that taste like the sea

The volcanic soil and the lack of rain have, over the centuries, produced an unlikely food culture. Cherry tomatoes so concentrated they taste almost like jam. White eggplants. Fava beans served as a warm puree with olive oil and capers. And Assyrtiko, a crisp white wine grown in low circular baskets on the ground so the vines can hold onto their grapes against the wind. Dinner on a cliff terrace, with all of these on a single plate, is the closest thing to a religious argument for slow travel I know.

05
Why the whitewash

Beauty, by public-health decree

The white houses of the Cyclades started as a health measure — lime wash kills bacteria, keeps surfaces cool in the sun, and during a cholera outbreak in the 1930s the Greek government made it mandatory on the islands. What began as a public-health order became, by accident, the most photographed aesthetic in Europe. It is a useful reminder that some of the most beautiful things in the world were originally just very practical.
