Five Stunning Places 05  /  Patagonia
No. 05

Patagonia, the end of the known world.

Chile & Argentina  ·  Southern Cone  ·  ~50°S, 73°W

Torres del Paine rising above a Patagonian lake.

01
The last place

A country-sized wilderness, split in two

Patagonia is not a country. It is a region that spans the southern ends of Chile and Argentina, an area about the size of western Europe with a population of roughly two million people — most of them concentrated in a handful of towns. South of that, it is mostly wind, sky, and sheep. The name was given by Magellan's crew in 1520 to describe a group of tall Tehuelche natives; the land they described was already acting the same way it acts today.

02
Torres del Paine

Three granite teeth

On the Chilean side, Torres del Paine National Park is built around three vertical granite spires that rise straight out of a glacial lake. Climbers have spent months trying to get up them; most visitors settle for the nine-hour round-trip hike to the viewpoint at their base, which in good weather rewards you with the most improbable sunrise of your life. In bad weather, which is most days, it rewards you with a new definition of the word “wind.”

03
The glaciers

Perito Moreno, 30 kilometers of moving ice

On the Argentine side, Perito Moreno Glacier is a five-kilometer-wide wall of blue ice that ends in a sixty-meter cliff above a milky lake. It is one of the last advancing glaciers on Earth — it creeps forward about two meters a day — and every few minutes, with a crack like a rifle shot, another building-sized block of ice calves off the face and crashes into the water. You stand on a boardwalk across the lake and watch it happen. There is nowhere to look that is not beautiful.

04
The wind

Weather that has opinions

Patagonian wind is a character, not a condition. It comes off the Andes, accelerates across the steppe, and arrives at you with enough force to flatten a tent or knock a grown adult off their feet. Locals describe four seasons per day as a conservative estimate. The upside is that the weather clears as quickly as it arrives — you can have a whiteout at breakfast and a cloudless blue sky by lunch, and the second one will feel earned.

05
Why you go

The argument for the end of the world

Most travel is about seeing new places. Patagonia is about being briefly reminded that the planet is much bigger than the human story happening on it. You walk for a day and see two other humans, four guanacos, and a condor. You camp under a sky that has not been light-polluted and realize you had forgotten how many stars there actually are. You come home quieter than you left. That is, I suspect, the whole point.

“I have seen nothing like it. No words are adequate to describe the loneliness of this land.” Bruce Chatwin, In Patagonia, 1977
